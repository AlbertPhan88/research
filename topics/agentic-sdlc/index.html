Research Notes / Agentic SDLC

The Agentic SDLC, per Anthropic and OpenAI

Study date: 14 October 2026 · Sources: 3 Anthropic primary, 1 OpenAI primary, 1 OpenAI secondary

Anthropic and OpenAI describe the same model. The classic SDLC stages stay. Agents do most of the execution inside each stage. Humans move to specification, harness design, verification, and final ownership of what ships.

Source: O1.

O1 also names concrete practices:

  • A committed PLAN.md for multi-step work.
  • An AGENTS.md that lists test and lint commands, so the agent has a feedback loop.
  • Tests written as a separate step and confirmed failing before implementation.
  • A review evaluation set built from gold-standard PRs. Review quality is measured by reactions to PR comments.

5. Harness engineering: where the method lives

Both labs' engineering posts locate capability in the harness, not the prompt. A harness is the environment, artifacts, and checks around the agent.

Findings from A3

  • Fact Self-evaluation is lenient. Agents praise their own work. A separate, skeptical evaluator was easier to build than a self-critical builder.
  • Fact The evaluator is not reliable out of the box. It found real issues, then talked itself into approving the work. Tuning it took several rounds of reading its logs.
  • Fact Quality costs time and money. A solo agent took 20 minutes and $9, but its core feature was broken. The full harness took 6 hours and $200 and produced a working app.
  • Fact Harnesses decay as models improve. With Opus 4.6, the team dropped sprints and moved the evaluator to a single final pass. An evaluator earns its cost only on tasks at the edge of what the model can do alone.
  • Fact Simplify one component at a time. A radical cut lost performance. Removing parts one by one worked.

7. How strong is the evidence?

  • Risk Vendor bias. Every source comes from a company that sells coding agents. The case studies (CRED “2x”, TELUS “30% faster”, Augment “4–8 months to 2 weeks”) are customer self-reports with no baselines.
  • Risk Predictions, not data. A1 calls itself “predictions … not certainties”. Only the 60% / 0–20% figure and the 27% figure come from Anthropic's internal research.
  • Inference The strongest evidence is the negative evidence. The failure modes are concrete and reproducible: premature completion, lenient self-grading, stubbed tests, and context decay. They are worth more than the throughput claims.
  • Assumption O2 is a greenfield case. It reportedly produced about 1M lines in five months with no human-written code, from a small team. That shows what a purpose-built repository allows. It says little about legacy codebases.
  • Fact O1 cites METR: as of August 2025, leading models sustained about 2 hours 17 minutes of work at 50% success, and task length doubles roughly every seven months.
  • Inference At a 50% success rate, verification cannot be optional. That explains why both labs invest so heavily in evaluators and tests.

8. What to adopt, in order

  1. Write CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md as a short map: build, test, and lint commands, plus pointers to deeper docs.
  2. Move decisions out of chat and into the repository, as plan files and decision records. The agent cannot see anything else.
  3. Write tests first, confirm that they fail, and forbid the agent from editing them.
  4. Do one feature per session and end in a mergeable state. Hand off through a progress file and the git log.
  5. Verify the way a user would, through a browser or end-to-end tests, not unit tests alone.
  6. Separate the builder from the grader with a review agent or a second model that uses an explicit rubric.
  7. Turn every recurring review complaint into a lint rule or a test.
  8. On each model upgrade, remove harness parts one at a time, and keep only those that still bear load.
